# The Last Line of Judgment

It is 3 a.m. at a university gate in Islamabad. A camera on the pole blinks red: *unregistered vehicle, erratic approach, possible threat.* The software has done its job perfectly. It has measured the car's speed, scanned its plate, and found no match.

The guard on duty, a man in a faded jacket who has been awake for eleven hours, walks to the window anyway. Inside is a young woman, shaking, her phone dead, her brother bleeding in the back seat. She took a wrong turn looking for a hospital.

The camera saw a threat. The guard saw a sister.

That gap is the whole answer to the question.

Machines are already better than us at watching. They do not blink, sleep, or get bored at hour nine of a twelve-hour shift. A drone can sweep a perimeter in minutes; an algorithm can spot an unattended bag in a crowd of thousands. Pretending otherwise is nostalgia, not argument. The guard who only stands and stares is already being replaced, and honestly, that guard was never given a fair job to begin with.

But watching is not the same as guarding. Guarding means deciding, and deciding means carrying the weight of being wrong. A machine can flag. It cannot apologise to a frightened mother. It cannot talk a drunk, angry man out of a fight with a joke and a cup of tea. It cannot stand in a courtroom and explain why it chose to act. When a decision can ruin a life, someone with a name and a conscience has to own it.

Air defence learned this long ago. Radar detects an aircraft in seconds, but a trained officer decides whether it is a threat or a lost airliner full of families. Technology widens the eyes; it does not replace the mind. Security on the ground deserves the same principle.

So yes, the guard of the future will be human — just not the one we underpay and overlook today. Picture instead a professional who commands the cameras rather than competing with them: trained to read fear and anger, to calm a crowd, to give first aid before the ambulance arrives, and to overrule a machine when it is confidently wrong. Getting there means paying for training, setting standards for the companies that hire guards, and writing one simple rule into every automated system: a human makes the final call.

Back at the gate, the barrier lifts. The guard radios ahead to the nearest hospital and points the way. The camera logs the event as *threat cleared.*

It will never know what really happened there. That is exactly why we still need someone who does.

The future guard will not be the last line of defence. They will be the last line of judgment, and judgment, for now and long after, is human.
