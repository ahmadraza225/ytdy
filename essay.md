# The Last Line of Judgment

*Will the Security Guard of the Future Be Human?*

Picture a university gate in Islamabad at three in the morning. Above the barrier, a camera that never blinks has been watching for six hours, and a screen in the guard room glows with boxes drawn around every moving thing. At 3:07 a car slows, stops, reverses, stops again. The software does what it was built to do. It draws a red box and raises an alert: *suspicious vehicle*.

The guard steps out of his cabin, torch in hand, and walks to the window. Inside is a young woman, shaking, phone dead, lost on her way to a hospital where her father has just been admitted. The machine saw a threat. The guard saw a daughter. He points her the right way, and she drives off toward the hospital. The red box disappears from the screen.

Nothing in that story is dramatic, and that is exactly why it matters. Most of security is not chases and alarms; it is a thousand small decisions about people. The question this essay asks is whether those decisions will still be made by a person. My answer is yes, but not by the person we picture today.

## The guard we overlook

In Pakistan, the security guard is everywhere and nowhere. He stands outside banks, schools, hospitals, petrol pumps, housing societies and offices. We pass him several times a day and rarely learn his name. Many guards work twelve-hour shifts, sometimes longer, for modest pay, after training that may last only days. Some carry weapons with little instruction on when not to use them.

We ask a great deal of these men and women. We expect them to stop a robbery, to calm an angry visitor, to notice the stranger who does not belong, to help when someone collapses in a corridor or a fire starts in a basement. Then we pay them as if their job were only to stand still.

## What machines already do better

A modern camera network can watch hundreds of entrances at once. Video analytics, software that reads camera footage and flags unusual patterns, can spot an unattended bag, a person climbing a fence, or a car circling the same block. Access control systems open doors only for the right card, fingerprint or face. Drones can patrol a perimeter in minutes that would take a guard an hour on foot. Pakistan is already moving this way: Islamabad's Safe City project links a large network of cameras to a central command centre. Yet when those cameras flag something, it is still a person who is sent to look.

These machines do not get tired, bored or bribed. For the job of *watching*, they are simply better than we are.

## The sceptic's case

So let us make the strongest argument against my position. Suppose the cameras get better every year, the algorithms learn from millions of hours of footage, and the robots become cheap. Why keep a human at the gate at all? A human is the most expensive, least reliable part of the system. Humans are biased, distracted and corruptible. Remove them, the sceptic says, and security becomes cheaper, fairer and more consistent.

Part of this is right. The guard whose only job is to stand and look *will* disappear, and few will miss a role that wastes a person's abilities. But the argument fails at its most important step. It assumes security is mostly detection, when detection is only where security begins. What matters is what happens next.

## What only a human can do

A camera can tell you a car has stopped. It cannot tell you why. It can recognise a face, but not fear, grief or drunken confusion. It can sound an alarm, but it cannot lower its voice to calm a crowd, or decide that a frightened teenager needs reassurance rather than a confrontation.

There is also a question no technology can answer: who is responsible? When a system wrongly flags an innocent person, when force is used, when someone is detained, a person must be able to explain and defend that decision. You cannot put an algorithm on trial. Accountability is not a technical feature that can be added later. It is the reason we trust security at all.

This is why I think of the future guard as *the last line of judgment*. Not the first line of defence, which machines will increasingly hold, but the final point where a human being looks at what the machines have found and decides what it means.

## A lesson from the sky

Studying at Air University, in a setting shaped by aviation and air defence, I have come to see this question differently. Air defence is one of the clearest examples of humans and machines sharing a security job. Radar and sensors watch the sky far beyond what any eye could see. Some defences now fire automatically against incoming rockets, but where the target might be a person, such as an unidentified aircraft, a trained human still decides, because the cost of a wrong call is too high.

The idea at the heart of this is often called a *force multiplier*: technology that lets a small number of skilled people do far more than they could alone. It does not make the person unnecessary. It makes the person's judgment more valuable, because each decision now carries more weight. The same principle can reshape ordinary security. One trained operator, supported by cameras, drones and analytics, can protect a campus better than ten untrained guards standing at ten gates, provided that operator is skilled, alert and trusted to decide.

## The risks we must not ignore

A future like this is not automatically a good one. If automation arrives without planning, Pakistan's large guard workforce could lose its livelihood with nowhere to go. Surveillance can quietly grow into something that watches citizens rather than protecting them. Facial recognition systems have been criticised for being less accurate for some groups of people than others, which means mistakes may fall hardest on those already treated unfairly. Systems can be hacked, cut off by a power failure, or fooled. Perhaps most dangerous is overconfidence: the habit of trusting the screen more than our own eyes.

There is a safety side too, which the security debate often forgets. A tired guard on a long night shift is a risk to himself and to others. A guard working alone has no one to help if he is hurt. And in a fire, an earthquake or a medical emergency, the guard is often the first trained person on the scene. A robot can raise the alarm; it cannot carry someone down a smoke-filled stairwell or hold a stranger's hand until the ambulance arrives.

## The guard of 2040

So what does the human guard of the future look like? Imagine her in 2040. She works in a control room and on foot. She can read the data from a dozen sensors, fly a drone, spot when an algorithm is wrong, and walk out into the night to talk to a frightened driver. She is trained in first aid, fire response and de-escalation, the skill of calming a tense situation before it turns violent. She is paid like the professional she is, and she has a career ahead of her: from guard, to control-room operator, to security supervisor.

Getting there will not happen on its own. Three steps would make it possible:

1. **Train the guards we already have.** Security companies, with government support, should offer recognised courses in technology, first aid and de-escalation, so automation becomes a ladder up rather than a door out.
2. **Set national standards.** Pakistan already licenses private security companies; those rules must now be updated and extended to AI security systems: who can use facial recognition, how data is stored, and how systems are tested for accuracy and bias.
3. **Keep a human in the loop.** Any decision to use force, detain a person or deny someone entry on the word of a machine should require a trained human to confirm it.

Who pays? Partly the companies, who will save money as machines take over routine watching, and partly the state, which benefits from safer public spaces and a skilled workforce. The cost of training is small next to the cost of one wrong decision made by a system no one is responsible for.

## Back at the gate

Return to that gate at three in the morning. In the future, there may be no cabin, no plastic chair, no torch. There will be more cameras, a drone overhead, and software far smarter than today's. And when it draws its red box around a car that has stopped in the dark, someone will still have to walk over, look through the window and decide.

Machines will keep watch. But only a human can judge what they have seen and answer for that decision, and that is why the security guard of the future will still be human.
