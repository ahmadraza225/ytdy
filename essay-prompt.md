MY POSITION (sharpen it, keep the spirit)
The future guard will be human, but not the guard we know today. The guard who only stands and watches will be replaced by cameras, drones, AI video analytics and patrol robots. The guard who reads people, judges, calms a situation and directs the technology will be worth more than ever. Machines detect; humans decide. Someone must answer for the decision, and you cannot put an algorithm on trial.

STAGE 2: DRAFT (only after I answer Stage 1)

Structure:
1. Opening scene, about 100 words. If my Stage 1 answers include a real moment at a gate, build the scene from that. If not, frame it clearly as imagined ("Picture a night at a university gate..."): the AI camera flags a car as suspicious, and the guard walks over to find a frightened, crying driver who is lost or in trouble. The machine saw a threat; the person saw a human being. Let the reader feel the question before it is asked. Never present an invented scene as something I saw.
2. Today's guard: what the job really involves, why it matters, and why it is undervalued in Pakistan (long hours, low pay, little training, fatigue, working alone).
3. The case for machines: what cameras, AI analytics, drones, patrol robots and smart access control already do well. Include the trade-offs: they never tire, but they cost money, break down, can be fooled and can be hacked.
4. The sceptic's case and my answer (from Stage 1), stated fairly before I rebut it.
5. What only a human can do: reading intent, empathy, calming a conflict, improvising, ethical judgment, public trust, and being accountable.
6. The air-defence lens: in air defence, radar and sensors spot a threat but a trained person makes the call. Use this as the model for security in general: technology strengthens the human instead of replacing them. Keep it to widely known principles only, with no operational or sensitive detail.
7. Risks: job losses across a large guard workforce, surveillance and privacy, bias in facial recognition, too much trust in automation, cyber attacks. Include the safety side: tired guards, lone workers, the guard as first responder in a fire or medical emergency.
8. The way forward: who pays to turn today's guard into tomorrow's trained, tech-literate, better-paid security professional, and why it is worth it. Frame upskilling as a career ladder (guard → control-room operator → security supervisor), not a consolation prize. End with three concrete recommendations: (a) training and upskilling for existing guards, (b) national standards for security companies and AI security systems, (c) a rule that a human makes the final call on any use of force or detention.
9. Closing: return to the guard at the gate and answer the title question directly in one memorable line.
